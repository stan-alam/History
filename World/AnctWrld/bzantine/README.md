\## Byzantine reading list



1\. The New Roman Empire: A History of Byzantium by Anthony Kaldellis



As the first full single-author history of Eastern Rome to appear in over a generation, ‘The New Roman Empire’ is a revolutionary piece of work. The book isn’t some dry chronicle; Kaldellis’s revisionist position and engaging prose make this thousand-page monograph surprisingly approachable. You’ll almost certainly walk away from this book with a fresh perspective and new ideas.



2\. The Fall of the Roman Empire: A New History of Rome and the Barbarians by Peter Heather



If you’re looking for a nuanced, informative, and accessible history of Western Rome’s decline, this book is for you! Heather does a terrific job of laying out all the major competing explanations for Western Rome’s collapse, while explaining exactly how and why the Eastern Empire carried on. Heather’s writing is dramatic as well as understandable, and I encourage any interested learners to pick up this book.



3\. Inventing Byzantine Iconoclasm by Leslie Brubaker



In ‘Inventing Byzantine Iconoclasm’, Brubaker does an incredible job with presenting new evidence, synthesizing decades of scholarship, and debunking old misconceptions on Eastern Roman iconoclasm. This book is a must-read for anyone interested in the 8th and 9th centuries!



4\. The Empire of Manuel I Komnenos 1143-1180 by Paul Magdalino



While an older text, there’s a reason why Magdalino’s ‘The Empire of Manuel I Komnenos’ remains the quintessential survey for the late Komnenian period. Comprehensive, accessible, and rigorous, this book is an absolute must-read for mid-late Byzantine history buffs.



5\. Surviving Rome: The Economic Lives of the Ninety Percent by Kim Bowes



Published less than a year ago, it has quickly become one of the most fascinating Roman economic histories out there. Though she doesn’t directly cover Eastern Rome in detail, Bowes utilizes revolutionary new technology to present a never-seen-before perspective on the economic reality for the average Roman. As an incredibly technical and interesting monograph, I believe it belongs on this list.
